# EzeCreditServices — Brand Voice & Content Guide

Use this document as the source of truth for SEO articles, pillar pages, FAQs, landing pages, and product copy. Every draft should sound like EzeCreditServices: plain-spoken, specific, calm, and honest about what tradelines can and cannot do.

## 1. Brand at a Glance

- **Business:** EzeCreditServices
- **Product:** Authorized user tradelines
- **Core promise:** Clear inventory, written posting guarantees, and real people available to answer questions.
- **Positioning:** A transparent, human alternative to hype-driven tradeline marketing.
- **Origin story:** Matt started the company in 2016 after a bad experience while buying his first home.
- **Human proof:** Customers can speak with Matt, Kathleen, and the team at the Raleigh office.

### Products and programs

- **Authorized user tradelines:** Buyers are added as authorized users to seasoned cards with positive payment history and low utilization. The standard term is two reporting cycles (about 60 days).
- **Extensions:** Customers can extend a line one reporting cycle at a time for half the original price, with no stated limit on extensions.
- **Supplier program:** Cardholders can earn money by making authorized-user spots available.
- **Broker program:** Eligible businesses can access wholesale pricing, with discounts of up to 20%.

**Commercial details to verify before publishing:** Inventory spans 10 major banks. The typical line costs about $450, with a stated range of roughly $200–$2,000. Do not present these as fixed prices or guaranteed availability; check current inventory and pricing.

## 2. Voice and Tone

**In one sentence:** Plain, calm, honest, and direct—especially when the honest answer is that a tradeline may not help.

### Voice principles

| Be | Avoid |
|---|---|
| Plain: use short sentences and everyday language | Dense jargon or “finance-bro” language |
| Calm: explain the facts without pressure | Countdown timers, “act now,” or manufactured urgency |
| Honest: explain limitations near the top | Vague claims or fine-print caveats |
| Direct: say when a tradeline is unlikely to help | Condescending or judgmental language |
| Specific: use dates, limits, reporting cycles, and clear examples | Empty adjectives such as “amazing” or “massive” |
| Human: use real names and contact details where appropriate | Faceless phrases such as “our team of experts” |

### Anchor sentence

Use this as the editorial test for every draft:

> “We don't guess how many points you'll gain. We guarantee, in writing, that your tradeline will post—and we'll tell you before you buy if it won't help.”

Only use the guarantee wording when it accurately matches the current written guarantee and applicable purchase conditions.

## 3. Writing Rules

1. **Use facts instead of hype.** Write “a 12-year-old Chase line with a $20,000 limit,” not “a powerful, aged tradeline.”
2. **Explain the mechanism before the benefit.** Walk readers through the statement date, reporting date, bureau update, and what they may see on their report.
3. **Put limitations near the top.** If the product is unlikely to help a reader, say so in the body copy—not only in a disclaimer.
4. **Invite a conversation, not an impulse purchase.** Prefer “talk to a real person” over “buy before it's gone.” Mention Matt, Kathleen, or the Raleigh office when relevant.
5. **Write for consumers and the professionals advising them.** A loan officer or credit professional should be able to forward the article to a client without rewriting it.
6. **Be firm on fraud and unrealistic claims.** Keep explanations about CPNs, score promises, and misleading credit claims short, clear, and unambiguous.
7. **Keep every claim supportable.** Distinguish what the company guarantees from what depends on card issuers, bureaus, scoring models, lenders, or a customer's wider credit file.

## 4. Positioning and Proof

### Core differentiator

EzeCreditServices shows key line details before payment—including bank, credit limit, account age, reporting date, and order-by date. The company states that each line comes with five written guarantees and a surety bond, and customers can contact real staff at its Raleigh office.

Verify current inventory displays, guarantee documents, bond details, and contact information before making these claims in published content.

### Proof points to draw on

- **Professional referrals:** More than 75% of sales reportedly come from referring professionals, including loan officers, real estate agents, credit repair firms, funding companies, and dealership finance managers. Verify this figure before publication.
- **Founder story:** Matt founded the company in 2016 after a frustrating experience while buying his first home.
- **Real people and a physical office:** The Raleigh office and reviews naming staff such as Matt, Kathleen, and Marc help make the business feel accountable.
- **Visible inventory:** Bank, limit, age, reporting date, and order-by date are available before payment.
- **Written guarantees and surety bond:** The posting guarantee requires purchase at least five days before the reporting date. Check the current terms before describing the guarantee.
- **Flexible extensions:** Customers can extend one cycle at a time for half the original price, subject to current terms.
- **Supplier program:** The company says supplier rates help it maintain inventory. Avoid comparative claims such as “among the highest in the industry” unless current evidence supports them.

### The customer problem

- **Practical problem:** A consumer has a thin or young credit file and is approaching a mortgage, auto loan, refinance, or other application deadline.
- **Trust problem:** The consumer may have been misled or disappointed by another tradeline company. Transparency and realistic expectations are central to the purchase decision.
- **What the product does:** An authorized user tradeline may add an existing account's reported history to a person's credit file. The exact reporting and scoring effects vary.
- **What it does not do:** It does not erase late payments, collections, or charge-offs; guarantee a score increase; or guarantee loan approval.

## 5. Audience Priorities

### 1. Referring professionals

Examples: loan officers, real estate agents, credit repair firms, funding companies, and dealership finance managers.

They care about deadlines, predictability, and protecting their client relationships. Discuss actual reporting dates, closing dates, auto deals, refinances, and funding timelines. Never imply that a tradeline guarantees approval or a successful closing.

### 2. Individuals with an upcoming application

Examples: people preparing for a mortgage, auto loan, or refinance, or people with a thin or young credit file.

Explain how reporting works, what details to compare, what may limit usefulness, and why no honest provider can promise a particular score change.

### 3. Suppliers

Cardholders who want to earn money by making authorized-user spots available. Explain the process, requirements, payments, and protections clearly, using current program terms.

### 4. Brokers

Businesses interested in wholesale pricing. Explain eligibility, the stated discount of up to 20%, and how the program works. Verify current terms before publication.

## 6. Important Topic Guidance

- **Timing matters.** The statement date and issuer reporting process matter more than the date a customer places an order. Missing a card's cycle can delay reporting by about a month.
- **Explain the five-day window.** Tie posting-timing content to the actual reporting date, order-by date, and current written guarantee.
- **A tradeline is not a universal solution.** Recent late payments, collections, charge-offs, and other factors may outweigh any benefit from an added authorized-user account.
- **Compare a line with the existing file.** The brand's guidance is to consider lines that are older and have a higher limit than accounts already on the buyer's file. Present this as guidance, not a guaranteed outcome.
- **Do not overstate long-term effects.** A tradeline is offered for a defined period, but what happens to an authorized user's credit file after removal depends on bureau reporting and other circumstances. Do not imply that benefits necessarily remain after the line is removed.
- **Do not promise points.** Scoring models and lender decisions vary. Explain the factors and uncertainty instead of estimating a score increase.

## 9. Claims and Language to Avoid

These are hard rules. Rewrite a draft that breaks them; do not soften the wording and leave the implication in place.

1. **No score promises or implied score ranges.** Never claim “boost your score 100 points,” “average gain of X,” or “jump to 700+.”
2. **No unsupported posting-time promises.** Explain the statement date, reporting date, and five-day purchase window, and accurately describe the written guarantee.
3. **No CPN or fake-identity promotion.** Do not sell, recommend, or normalize CPN schemes or language about creating a new credit identity. Explain the risks plainly.
4. **No claim that tradelines fix bad credit.** They do not remove late payments, collections, or charge-offs and are not a substitute for credit repair.
5. **No hidden-inventory framing.** Do not say customers must contact the company to discover basic line details if those details are shown before payment.
6. **No manufactured urgency.** Avoid “only two left,” arbitrary price deadlines, and countdowns. Mention urgency only when it comes from a real order-by date or customer deadline.
7. **No universal-benefit claims.** Explain who may not benefit and why.
8. **No hype in place of evidence.** Avoid “massive,” “guaranteed approval,” “skyrocket,” “secret,” “hack,” and “loophole.”
9. **No competitor bashing.** Contrast transparent practices with common industry practices without naming or attacking competitors.
10. **No faceless corporate voice.** Use real staff names when appropriate instead of generic claims about “our team of experts.”
11. **No unqualified claims about legality or bureau policy.** Explain the specific practice and cite current authoritative sources where needed.
12. **No unsupported comparative claims.** Claims such as “highest supplier rates in the industry” require current, verifiable evidence.

## 10. Compliance and Fact-Check Checklist

Before publishing, check the following:

- [ ] Score changes, approvals, and lending outcomes are never promised or implied.
- [ ] Timing explanations accurately distinguish purchase date, statement date, reporting date, and bureau update.
- [ ] The five-day guarantee and all guarantee language match current written terms.
- [ ] Bank, limit, account age, reporting date, and order-by date details match the actual inventory.
- [ ] Pricing, extension costs, supplier terms, and broker discounts are current.
- [ ] Legal, regulatory, FTC, and bureau-policy claims are supported by current authoritative sources.
- [ ] The copy explains who may not benefit, including people with significant negative items in their files.
- [ ] Claims about what happens after an authorized user is removed are qualified and fact-checked.
- [ ] Testimonials, referral statistics, surety-bond statements, and competitor comparisons are substantiated.
- [ ] The call to action invites a conversation or helps the reader evaluate fit without pressure.

### Internal caution: closed accounts and removal

In many cases, when an authorized user is removed, the account may be deleted from that person's credit file. Do not state as a universal rule that a score or benefit will remain after removal. Any example involving a score built on closed accounts must identify the specific circumstances, bureau, scoring model, and evidence—or be reviewed for compliance before publication.

### Internal caution: comparative claims

Claims such as “among the highest supplier rates in the industry” need evidence that can be produced and reviewed. If evidence is unavailable, remove the comparison or describe the program without ranking it against competitors.

## Final Editorial Test

Before approving any content, ask:

1. Is it clear and useful to someone who has never bought a tradeline?
2. Does it explain how the process works before describing potential benefits?
3. Are the limitations and uncertainties stated plainly and early?
4. Can every factual and commercial claim be supported?
5. Would a professional feel comfortable forwarding this to a client?
6. Does the page sound like a knowledgeable person explaining the facts—not a salesperson promising an outcome?
