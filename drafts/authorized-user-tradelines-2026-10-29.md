# Authorized User Tradelines: How It Works and Who It May Not Help

**Short version:** An authorized user tradeline is an existing credit card account, with its own history, that you're added to as an authorized user. If the issuer reports authorized users to the bureaus, the account may appear on your credit report. It can add history to a thin or young file. It does not erase late payments, collections, or charge-offs, it does not guarantee a score increase, and it does not guarantee a loan approval.

## What is an authorized user?

An authorized user is someone added to another person's credit card account. They may or may not get a physical card, and they are not responsible for paying the bill. The primary cardholder is. People have done this for years: parents add children, spouses add each other.

A **tradeline** is simply the credit bureaus' name for an account on a credit report. An **authorized user tradeline**, in the sense this site uses it, is a seasoned card account with positive payment history and low utilization that a buyer is added to for a set period.

You'll also hear "piggybacking" or "piggyback credit." Same idea.

## How an authorized user tradeline gets onto your report

The sequence matters more than anything else on this page, because most confusion about "when will it post" comes from skipping it.

1. **You're added** to the cardholder's account.
2. **The statement closes.** Each card has a statement date every month.
3. **The issuer reports** the account's balance and status to the bureaus. This is the reporting date. It's set by the issuer, not by you or us.
4. **The bureaus update** your file. Not all issuers report to all three bureaus, and bureaus update on their own schedule.
5. **You may see it** on your credit report.

Because everything keys off the statement and reporting dates, the day you place an order matters less than whether you ordered before the card's cycle. Miss the cycle and reporting can slip by about a month. That's why we show the reporting date and order-by date on every line before payment, and why our written posting guarantee requires purchase at least five days before the reporting date.

The standard term is two reporting cycles, about 60 days.

## What it can do

- Add an older account to a young file.
- Add available credit, which can lower your overall utilization on paper.
- Add positive payment history to a file that has little of it.

"Can" is the right word. How much a scoring model weighs an authorized user account varies by model, by lender, and by what else is on your file. Some lenders look at authorized user accounts differently than others, and some manual underwriting reviews discount them. We don't guess how many points anyone will gain, and you should be wary of anyone who does.

## What it does not do

- It does **not** remove late payments, collections, or charge-offs.
- It does **not** guarantee a score increase.
- It does **not** guarantee loan approval or a successful closing.
- It is **not** credit repair.

## Who it may not help

This is the part many sellers skip.

**Recent negative items.** If you have recent late payments, collections, or charge-offs, one more positive account may not outweigh them. Address those first.

**A file that already has stronger accounts.** If your oldest card is 15 years old with a high limit, a line that is younger or smaller adds nothing. Our guidance is to consider lines that are older and have a higher limit than what's on your file.

**High utilization on your own cards.** Paying down your balances may help more than any added account.

**Someone who needs a result by a date that has already passed.** If your lender needs documents next week and the next reporting date is five weeks out, a tradeline won't arrive in time. We'd rather tell you that than take the order.

**Someone hoping for a permanent change.** When an authorized user is removed, the account may be deleted from that person's credit file. What happens to your scores after that depends on the bureau, the scoring model, and the rest of your file. Don't count on a benefit staying after removal.

## Authorized user vs. primary tradelines vs. CPNs

- **Authorized user tradeline:** You're added to an existing account. You don't own the account or its debt.
- **Primary tradeline:** An account opened in your own name. That's an ordinary credit account, not something you buy.
- **CPN (credit privacy number):** Sometimes sold as a way to start a "new credit identity." The FTC has warned about these. Using a number that isn't your own Social Security number on credit applications can be fraud. We don't sell them, recommend them, or consider them an alternative to anything on this page.

## Is it legal?

Being added as an authorized user to someone's card is a legal, common arrangement. How a tradeline is bought and used is what matters: be accurate on any application, and don't represent yourself as something you're not. Lenders' policies on authorized user accounts also differ. If you have questions about your specific situation, a lender or attorney can answer them.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long does a tradeline stay on my report?**
The standard term is two reporting cycles, about 60 days. You can extend one cycle at a time for half the original price, subject to current terms.

**How many bureaus will it report to?**
It depends on the issuing bank. Check before you buy.

**How many points will my score go up?**
We don't know and neither does anyone else. It depends on the scoring model, the lender, and your whole file.

**Do I need more than one?**
Not necessarily. A single line that is older and higher-limit than your existing accounts is a better test than a count of lines.

**Can I be the cardholder instead?**
Yes. See our supplier program, where cardholders can earn money by making authorized-user spots available.
